# The Power-Source-Filter Model

This model describes the essential functioning of voice. It explains a bit of why we train the way we do, and how to understand the different components of training. It will not tell you when, where, how, or why to train.

## Power: Breathing is the foundation of your voice.

Most people have been breathing for their whole lives, so it's natural to suppose (wrongly) this should be a non-problem.

When you speak (or sing, or yell) your breathing supplies the input power. Changing your voice places different demands on that power source. Therefore, expect to spend some time working on breath control.

Controlled breathing can also be a good technique for managing stress. Although stress management is not our primary focus, many people do have strong feelings around their voice. A few "box-breaths" before and after a practice session can thus have many benefits.

## Source: Glottal behavior turns breath into sound.

Your glottis, the structure within the larynx consisting mainly of your vocal cords and the movable cartilages to which they attach, is the source of "voiced" sounds - the ones you need for all vowels and also for consonants like `b v m d z n g ng` and several others - but not for whispers or sounds like `h s f`.

This initial sound can vary significantly in pitch, volume, and so-called "vocal weight", helping to shape people's perceptions of your voice, so mastering control over the glottis is essential. However, this is not the final sound people hear.

Pitch and weight correlate - not in lock step, but they are connected as if by an elastic band: The very lowest pitches demand a heavier sound, while higher pitches are much more accessible with a lighter sound.


## Filter: The rest of the vocal tract shapes the sound dramatically.

Sound from the glottis initially enters the pharynx (throat). With proper technique, you can alter its length and shape to enhance different frequency ranges.

You already know how to move your tongue and jaw to form different vowels and consonants. However, you can adjust your tongue's baseline posture and range of motion to create the impression of a larger or smaller mouth.

Taken together, your pharynx and mouth contribute to what is called "vocal size", which is the most potent influence on vocal gender perception.

## Wrap-up: Putting everything in balance

If you change size, weight, and pitch in a nicely balanced manner, then you control people's perceptions of your sexual development. This, combined with region-specific adjustments to your accent (pronunciation and prosody) give the complete picture of an ordinary member of one or another gender.

Unbalanced changes, such as small-and-heavy or large-and-light, lead to a more cartoonish sound. Cartoons do still have gender, but listeners will infer that gender from secondary features like accent.

It may be tempting to want to just move right toward your target voice by changing everything at once. However, this is probably not wise. A realistic altered voice requires a careful coordination of features you may not yet be used to moving at all, much less coordinating.

At the early stages of voice training, it's very worthwhile to explore a wide variety of cartoonish sounds. They will help you gain control and coordination over your voice, but also helps keep the process fun.

When you have strong and smooth intentional control over, and isolation of, the major vocal features, you can effectively develop the coordination to do any voice you like whenever you like.
